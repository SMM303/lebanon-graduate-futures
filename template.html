Lebanon trains graduates for the world, and keeps too few of them.

Youth unemployment doubled between 2018-19 and January 2022, almost half of university-educated adults say they want to leave, and money sent home by emigrants now equals a third of the economy. This page sets out the evidence, how solid each number is, and what changes if Lebanon responds.

EVIDENCE 1 · MEASUREMENT

Global data cannot see the crisis

The ILO modelled estimates used in international dashboards show Lebanon's unemployment barely moving: {{mod_unemp_22}}% in 2022, below the {{mod_unemp_19}}% of 2019. The national survey counted {{unemp_22}}% that January {{b_unemp_22}}, and {{youth_22}}% for people aged 15-24.

For young people the modelled figure ({{mod_youth_22}}%) is {{youth_ratio_pct}}% of the survey figure, a gap of {{youth_gap_pts}} points. Anyone ranking countries from global data sees half of Lebanon's youth crisis.

Youth unemployment covers everyone aged 15-24 in the labour force. It is not a graduate unemployment rate, which no source currently publishes.

EVIDENCE 2 · DEGREES WITHOUT JOBS

More students, a smaller economy

Gross tertiary enrolment rose from {{tert_18}}% in 2018 to {{tert_22}}% in 2022. Over the same years GDP per capita fell {{gdp_fall}}%, reaching US${{gdp_23}} in 2023 before a partial recovery to US${{gdp_24}} in 2024. The survey employment-to-population ratio dropped from {{epr_19}}% to {{epr_22}}%.

University has become a waiting room. Youth labour underutilisation reached {{under_22}}% {{b_under_22}}, and {{ltu_22}}% of the unemployed had been looking for a year or more.

EVIDENCE 3 · THE DEGREE AS AN EXIT TICKET

The more educated, the readier to leave

In 2024, {{em_univ}}% of university-educated Lebanese wanted to emigrate, against {{em_less}}% of the less educated. Among ages 18-29 the figure is {{em_young}}%, double the {{em_old}}% of older adults.

The wish to leave has risen from {{em_2018}}% of all adults in 2018 to {{em_all}}% in 2024.

Arab Barometer measures intentions among Lebanese citizens, not recorded departures. It covers a different population from the CAS/ILO labour survey, so the two are never combined here.

EVIDENCE 4 · THE DIASPORA ECONOMY

Emigrants are the steadiest part of the economy

Remittances stayed between US${{rem_min}} and {{rem_max}} billion a year while GDP fell from US${{gdpusd_18}} billion (2018) to US${{gdpusd_23}} billion (2023). Their weight rose from {{rem_pct_18}}% to {{rem_pct_23}}% of GDP, about {{rem_ratio}} times Jordan's share in the same year.

This is both the risk and the opening: graduates who left are already Lebanon's largest source of foreign money. Hover a grey line to see which comparator it is.

EVIDENCE 5 · INVESTMENT AND TALENT LEFT IDLE

High enrolment, low public investment, women outside the market

Lebanon enrols {{tert_lbn}}% of the tertiary age group ({{tert_lbn_y}}), more than any Arab comparator shown, yet spends {{edu_lbn}}% of GDP on public education, about a third of the world average.

In January 2022 only {{lfp_f_22}}% of women were in the labour force, against {{lfp_m_22}}% of men. Women are the largest pool of trained talent the economy does not use.

ANALYTICS · WHAT MOVES YOUTH UNEMPLOYMENT

Faster growth goes with lower youth unemployment

A fixed-effects regression across Lebanon, Jordan, Egypt, Tunisia, Morocco and Cyprus ({{m_y0}}-{{m_y1}}, {{m_n}} country-years) links each extra point of GDP per capita growth to {{m_growth}} points lower youth unemployment (p = {{m_growth_p}}).

Remittances carry a positive sign ({{m_rem}} points per point of GDP) but the confidence interval crosses zero (p = {{m_rem_p}}), so the panel does not support a firm link. Higher female participation goes with higher youth unemployment (+{{m_lfp}} points, p = {{m_lfp_p}}), a pattern these aggregate data cannot explain. Filled dots are significant at 5%; hollow dots are not.

These are associations in modelled data, not causes. The ILO series is partly estimated from GDP, and Evidence 1 shows it misses Lebanon's crisis. The model should be refitted on survey data when more rounds exist.

Move the levers and follow five graduate cohorts

STRATEGIC READING

Strengths to build on, opportunities to take

Strengths

  • High tertiary enrolment: {{tert_lbn}}% in {{tert_lbn_y}}, above every Arab comparator shown.
  • Research output held up: {{art_10}} scientific articles in 2010, {{art_23}} in 2023.
  • A diaspora sending US${{rem_usd_23}} billion in 2023, while GDP shrank.
  • Multilingual graduates suited to remote service work.

Opportunities

  • A graduate outcomes observatory built from open data and a tracer survey with a partner university.
  • Diaspora hiring and mentoring networks for graduates who stay.
  • Remote-export jobs that earn foreign currency without emigration.
  • Bringing more women into work: participation was {{lfp_f_22}}% in January 2022, against {{lfp_m_22}}% for men.
