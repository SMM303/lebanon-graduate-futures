Lebanon Graduate Futures
MSBA 325 project proposal · Lebanese university graduates

Lebanon trains graduates for the world, and keeps too few of them.

Youth unemployment doubled in three years, almost half of university-educated adults want to leave, and the money emigrants send home now equals a third of the economy. This page sets out the evidence, the gaps in it, and what changes if Lebanon responds.

EVIDENCE 1 · MEASUREMENT

The global data cannot see the crisis

The ILO modelled estimates used in international dashboards show Lebanon's unemployment barely moving: 11.1% in 2022, lower than in 2019. The national survey counted 29.6% that January, and 47.8% for young people.

Anyone ranking countries from global data underestimates Lebanon's youth crisis by about half. The most recent national survey in our sources dates from January 2022.

EVIDENCE 2 · DEGREES WITHOUT JOBS

More students, a smaller economy

Gross tertiary enrolment rose from 47.2% in 2018 to 58.2% in 2022 while GDP per capita fell 62% to US$3,478 in 2023. The employment-to-population ratio dropped from 43.3% to 30.6%.

University has become a waiting room. Youth labour underutilisation reached 64.2%, and 48.8% of the unemployed had been looking for more than a year.

EVIDENCE 3 · THE DEGREE AS AN EXIT TICKET

The more educated, the readier to leave

In 2024, 46% of university-educated Lebanese wanted to emigrate, against 33% of the less educated. Among ages 18–29 the figure is 58%, double that of older adults.

Economic conditions drive 72% of would-be migrants. Their first choices are Canada, Germany and France, which compete hardest for skilled labour.

EVIDENCE 4 · THE DIASPORA ECONOMY

Emigrants are the steadiest part of the economy

Remittances held between US$6.4 and 7.4 billion a year while GDP fell from US$54.9 billion (2018) to US$20.1 billion (2023). Their weight rose from 12.7% to 33.3% of GDP, about four times Jordan's share.

EVIDENCE 5 · INVESTMENT AND TALENT LEFT IDLE

High enrolment, low public investment, women outside the market

Lebanon enrols a larger share of young people in tertiary education than its neighbours, yet spends 1.2% of GDP on public education, a third of the world average.

Only 22% of women are in the labour force, against 67% of men. Women are the largest pool of trained talent the economy does not use.

ANALYTICS · WHAT MOVES YOUTH UNEMPLOYMENT

Growth lowers it; dependence on remittances does not

A fixed-effects regression across Lebanon, Jordan, Egypt, Tunisia, Morocco and Cyprus links each extra point of GDP per capita growth to 0.35 points lower youth unemployment. Each extra point of remittances in GDP goes with 0.19 points higher.

Read these as associations in modelled data, not causes. The ILO series is partly estimated from GDP, and Exhibit 1 shows it misses Lebanon's crisis. The project would refit the model on survey data.

SCENARIO CONSOLE · WHAT IF LEBANON RESPONDS?

Move the levers and watch five graduate cohorts

A transparent what-if tool, not a forecast. Defaults use the survey youth unemployment rate and a stated assumption for annual graduates and departures; change any of them.

Starting conditions

Assumption. CRDP counted 222,064 tertiary students in 2019–20; a 4-year cycle implies about 55,000 a year.
Assumption. 46% of graduates say they want to leave; no national count of actual departures exists.
Default: CAS/ILO youth rate, January 2022.

Response measures

Jobs filled by graduates through remote work for foreign clients, start-ups and diaspora-owned firms.
Percentage points off the departure rate: career services, paid internships, return grants.
Points off the stayers' unemployment rate: curricula matched to demand, work-based learning, tracer data guiding programmes.

Baseline = no measures. Simple stock model: each cohort splits into emigrants and stayers; stayers are employed at (1 − unemployment + skills-alignment points) plus new programme jobs, capped at the number still unemployed. No attrition, return migration or wage effects are modelled.

STRATEGIC READING

Gaps, strengths and opportunities

Gaps

  • No graduate tracer data: nobody publishes what happens to graduates by university or field.
  • No national labour force survey after January 2022 in our sources; global modelled data understate youth unemployment by half.
  • No count of who actually emigrates, only stated intentions.
  • Public education spending at 1.2% of GDP.

Strengths

  • High tertiary enrolment: 54.4% in 2023, above every Arab peer shown.
  • Research output held up: 776 scientific articles in 2010, 2,079 in 2023.
  • A diaspora sending US$6.7 billion a year, concentrated in skilled economies.
  • Multilingual graduates suited to remote service work.

Opportunities

  • A graduate outcomes observatory built from open data and a tracer survey.
  • Diaspora hiring and mentoring networks in Canada, Germany and France.
  • Remote-export jobs that earn foreign currency without emigration.
  • Bringing more women into work: participation is 22%, against 67% for men.
